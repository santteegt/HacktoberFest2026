## Definition

Touring car aerodynamics is the set of adjustments made to the body shell and rear wing that change where downforce acts and how much of it survives to the track: the shell's fore/aft mounting position, its front lip clearance, the stoppers that limit how far it travels relative to the chassis, and the wing's angle and position. Unlike the geometry settings in [Touring Car Steering Geometry](touring-car-steering-geometry.md) and [Touring Car Suspension Tuning](touring-car-suspension-tuning.md), these are set with body posts, shims and reamed holes rather than turnbuckles, which makes them easy to treat as bodywork rather than as setup. The sources treat them as setup, with a named failure mode for getting the stoppers wrong.

## Key Points

- **Fore/aft body position**: mounting the shell forward, in a cited range of 2.0–6.0 mm, puts more of the splitter and hood ahead of the front wheels, increasing front downforce, sharpening turn-in and adding front steering response. Mounting it back shifts downforce toward the rear axle for stability through fast sweepers. This is already listed as a grip lever outside the fixed bench sequence on [Touring Car Setup Procedure](touring-car-setup-procedure.md); the mechanism is that the nose reaches the turn-in point earlier.
- **Front lip clearance**: 7.0–8.0 mm on outdoor asphalt and European carpet, and 9.0–10.0 mm only on high-drag CRC black carpet. Set above 8.0 mm on asphalt the car loses front downforce and grip, turning lazy and pushing in slow corners. Set too low the lip drags under braking, adding drag and unbalancing the chassis.
- **Body stoppers and up-stops**: adjustable aluminium horns or set screws, mounted on the steering arms or upper arms, that contact the inside of the front wheel arches at the limit of suspension compression. They stop the tyres scrubbing the inner arches and the splitter dragging, which lets the lip run down to 6.0 mm or lower. Adjust with shims or screw length so they touch only at the very end of travel, and leave a small gap between the body clip and the shell so it can float.
- **The named failure mode**: stoppers set too high hold the shell against the chassis at rest, turning it into a rigid four-point mount. The body then stays level while the chassis compresses underneath, which both removes the visual roll feedback a driver reads the car by and causes a severe loss of front grip and slow-corner steering. This is the first of the five common setup errors recorded on [Touring Car Setup Procedure](touring-car-setup-procedure.md), here with its mechanism spelled out.
- **Wing angle and position**: moving the wing forward, or running a lower and flatter angle, decreases rear downforce and adds corner-exit steering. Moving it back or raising it increases rear downforce and high-speed rear traction. Larger wing buttons, or rear body-post support braces, stop the rear deck and wing flexing or collapsing under aerodynamic load at speed.
- **Wheelbase and track width sit alongside these as chassis-level geometry with the same "set once at the bench" character**: shimming the front arms backward to shorten the wheelbase has the same steering effect as adding 1.0 mm of Ackermann shim at the rack, and a 0.5 mm rearward arm shift needs 0.5 mm added under the lower shock stud to keep the shock vertical. Narrowing the rear track with thinner hexes (4.3 or 4.5 mm) adds rear traction and side bite on low grip; widening the front track with thicker hexes (4.5 or 5.0 mm) calms a darty front end.
- **Body mounting is also a build-quality item on the owner's chassis**: the BD12's rear body-mount post spacing is 106 mm (53 mm off centreline), and the recommended order is to cut the shell's moulded rear cut line first, then align Yokomo's reinforcement patches to that line before reaming — see [Yokomo BD12](yokomo-bd12.md).
