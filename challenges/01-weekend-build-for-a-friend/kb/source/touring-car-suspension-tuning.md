## Definition

Touring car suspension tuning is the set of mechanisms — anti-roll bars, roll center geometry, spring/damper selection, shock mounting, and deliberate chassis flex — that control how much a car rolls in a corner and how fast that roll arrives, as distinct from [droop](touring-car-setup-procedure.md), which controls how far a wheel is allowed to travel. Where [Vehicle Dynamics Fundamentals](vehicle-dynamics-fundamentals.md) states that springs set how much weight transfer happens and damper oil sets how fast it arrives, this page is the mechanism-level detail behind that claim, plus the physical geometry (roll center, ARB) that sits alongside it.

## Key Points

- **Anti-roll bars (ARB)** transfer load between left and right suspension arms — free to rotate when both arms compress equally (straight-line bumps), stiffening only the loaded side under cornering roll. A thicker front bar sharpens turn-in and high-speed steering but adds edginess (baseline ~1.3 mm front); a thicker rear bar reduces off-power rotation and on-power rear traction, while a thinner rear bar increases both (baseline ~1.2 mm rear). The bar must sit flat and move freely in its bulkheads with a small amount of play — over-tightening the retaining collars binds the bar and strips out mechanical grip (see the [X4 common-error list](touring-car-setup-procedure.md)). Tweak check: disconnect shocks, caliper under the driveshaft/hex, adjust the ball-stud turnbuckle until both sides lift the opposite arm at the same mm reading.
- **Roll center** is the geometric point the chassis rolls around, set by the angle of the lower arms and upper camber links projecting to an "instant point"; a line from the tire contact patch through that point crosses the centerline at the roll-center height. Raising it (higher arm pins, or lower shims under the inner camber-link ball studs) gives immediate initial bite and less roll — good for tight, technical tracks. Lowering it gives a smoother roll and traction that builds later in the corner, reducing traction-rolling risk on high-grip surfaces.
- **Springs**: soft (2.5–2.6 linear) increases roll and side-bite in sweepers and reduces tire overheating on hot tracks; hard (3.0–3.2) sharpens initial bite on low-grip/dusty surfaces at the cost of bouncing on bumps; progressive springs (e.g. 2.5–2.8, or the Yokomo BD11's soft 2.0–2.3 progressive rear standard) start soft and stiffen under load. Stick to one manufacturer's line so rate steps stay comparable.
- **Shock oil viscosity**: typically 300–600 cSt, most commonly 400–500 cSt. Thinner oil speeds roll and initial side-bite (pair with harder springs on low-traction/dusty tracks); thicker oil slows weight transfer for a calmer car (pair with softer springs on high-grip carpet to avoid traction rolling). Stick to one oil brand — cSt ratings aren't standardized across manufacturers.
- **Piston and shaft position**: 4-hole pistons are the current asphalt-kit standard. A shorter shock shaft (~9 mm) sits the piston lower, leaving more oil above it for higher hydraulic pack on compression (stronger initial bite, faster rebound); a longer shaft (~11 mm) sits it higher, leaving more oil below for more rebound resistance (smoother direction changes on high grip).
- **Shock mounting angle**: standing shocks upright (outer tower holes) stiffens the initial stroke, reduces roll, and speeds weight transfer — standing the rear shocks up adds corner-exit rear traction. Laying shocks down (inner holes) softens the initial stroke while stiffening progressively deeper into travel, adding mid-corner side-bite and reducing traction-rolling risk.
- **Shock shimming**: spacers between the shock's ball studs and the tower/arm (baseline: 4.0 mm top front tower / 1.5 mm lower front arm; 3.0 mm top rear tower / 0 mm lower rear arm) add mounting-screw leverage and let the carbon tower flex slightly, softening damping feel and freeing chassis rotation — useful for taking edginess out of a nose-heavy car. Shifting suspension arms back for more grip requires adding matching shim to the lower shock mount to keep the shock vertical.
- **Chassis flex** is a deliberate, tunable grip lever, not just structural give: removing inner top-deck screws, using a thinner 1.6 mm top deck (vs. 2.0 mm stock), removing front motor-mount screws, or fitting split (rather than solid) front suspension blocks all increase flex and mechanical grip on loose/dusty asphalt. Less flex (stiffer chassis) sharpens responsiveness on high-grip carpet. Flex accidentally locked out — an over-tightened battery holder or motor-mount screw, or radio steering EPA set so the linkage presses on the caster block at full lock — strips mechanical grip as an unintended fault, not a deliberate setting; see the [common setup errors](touring-car-setup-procedure.md).
- **Camber-link length and ball-stud height are the two levers behind camber gain**, and they move roll centre at the same time. A shorter upper link swings through a tighter arc relative to the lower arm, increasing camber gain (a static 2.0 degrees negative growing to 2.5–2.7 degrees as the chassis rolls), which keeps the contact patch flatter in roll and generates more lateral side bite; a longer link reduces the rate of change for a smoother, more progressive, less edgy feel. Lowering the inner ball stud (fewer shims) or raising the outer one steepens the link, raising the roll centre and increasing camber gain for immediate initial bite in hairpins and tight chicanes; raising the inner stud (more shims) or lowering the outer one flattens it, lowering the roll centre and reducing gain so the chassis rolls more smoothly, turn-in softens, side bite arrives later in the corner, and traction-rolling risk on high grip drops.
- **Active Rear Suspension toe gain is a shimmed, quantified range rather than an on/off option.** ARS replaces fixed rear uprights with rear steering hubs driven by an adjustable toe link anchored to the inner bulkheads, so rear toe changes as the suspension compresses. Fewer shims under the inner block (a 1.0 mm shim is the example) angle the link upward toward the outside, so compression shortens it through its arc and pulls the upright inward: high toe gain, 3.0 degrees static rising to 3.8–4.0 degrees, locking the rear axle under power for markedly more corner-exit traction and stability. More shims (3.0 mm) level the link parallel to the arm, so compression produces almost no arc change and toe gains only about 0.3 degrees (3.0 to 3.3), freeing the rear to rotate around the apex for higher corner speed on technical tracks. The hardware also adds roughly 20 g directly to the rear axle, shifting balance rearward on its own even when set for zero gain — see [Touring Car Weight Balance](touring-car-weight-balance.md).
- **Rear suspension mount inserts** (A, B, C and D blocks) are the mechanism the sources describe for tuning rear roll centre, track width and anti-squat, rather than an adjustable arm sweep angle; shims under the rear pin mounts alter anti-squat and lower roll-centre height.
- **Shock build/maintenance**: lightly oil cartridge O-rings and shock-body threads before assembly so the seal cap doesn't tear the seal; when bleeding, pull the shaft down and compress slowly to avoid pushing oil out or trapping air bubbles. Turnbuckle threads (steering and suspension links alike) should be greased before threading into plastic ball cups, to keep trackside camber/toe adjustment smooth without popping cups off studs.
